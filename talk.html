Notes

Ginseng is Korea's plant; the old name for Korea, Goryeo, is where "Korean" comes from, and Koryo ginseng was prized across Asia for centuries. Wisconsin grows almost all of America's: about 90 to 95 percent, nearly all of it in Marathon County. Most of it goes to China and Hong Kong. Korea protects its own growers hard: in the 2012 free-trade agreement, Korea's starting tariff on red ginseng was 754 percent, and 222.8 percent on raw ginseng, with special safeguards. That's how much ginseng means to Korea. A Wausau grower, Hsu's, even grows "Korean ginseng" here from Korean seed.

Two Wisconsin Medals of Honor.

Red Cloud, Ho-Chunk, held his ground until he was killed. Ingman came home and made paper for thirty-two years.

Notes

Cpl. Mitchell Red Cloud Jr., Ho-Chunk, from Hatfield. November 5, 1950, in what is now North Korea: he saw the Chinese attack coming and sounded the alarm. Wounded, he braced himself against a tree and kept firing so his company could hold. He was twenty-five.

1 · Why Korea matters

Korea printed with metal type 78 years before Gutenberg.

Cheongju, where it was printed, makes SK hynix memory chips today.

Notes

In a paper town, start with paper. Japan's oldest chronicle records a Korean monk, Damjing, arriving in 610 and making paints, paper and ink. In the 1200s Korea carved more than 81,000 woodblocks of the Buddhist scriptures; they are still at Haeinsa temple. Then in July 1377, at Heungdeok Temple outside Cheongju, monks printed this book from cast metal type. Gutenberg's Bible came 78 years later. The only surviving copy is in Paris: a French diplomat in Seoul, Victor Collin de Plancy, took it home, and it has been in the national library there since 1952. Cheongju comes back in a few minutes, on the chip map.

1 · Why Korea matters

Buddhism, Confucianism, shamanism. No wars of religion.

Notes

For centuries Koreans lived with all three at once: Buddhist temples, Confucian family rites, and the shaman for when things go wrong. Europe fought the Thirty Years' War over religion; Korea mostly didn't. The worst exception was 1866, when the regent had thousands of Catholics killed, along with seven French priests. That was politics: Russian ships on the coast, French gunboats in Vietnam. And Korean Buddhism is world class. The Jikji you just saw is a Zen book. In 1972 a Korean monk, Seung Sahn, settled in Providence, Rhode Island, and his Kwan Um School now has Zen centers across America and abroad. The royal rite in the middle photo was performed this May, in Seoul.

1 · Why Korea matters

One royal house ruled Korea for 518 years.

Notes

Joseon began a hundred years before Columbus sailed and ended after the Wright brothers flew. The same royal house, the Yi, reigned from 1392 to 1910; in 1897 it renamed the country the Korean Empire, and Japan ended it in 1910. The Tudors managed 118 years, the Romanovs 304. Our own republic is 250 this year. This is the Korea the next two slides happen to: invaded in 1592, traded away in 1905.

Everyone here learned about the Spanish Armada. Four years later, Hideyoshi sent more than five times as many men across the strait to Korea. The plan was to go through Korea and conquer Ming China. It became a seven-year war. China sent 166,700 troops over its course, and Admiral Yi Sun-sin's navy cut the Japanese supply lines at sea. 1 · Why Korea matters

Two empires went to war over Korea. The peace was signed in New Hampshire.

Theodore Roosevelt won the Nobel Peace Prize. Japan got Korea.

Notes

Japan fought China over Korea in 1894 and won. It fought Russia over Korea and Manchuria in 1904 and won again. Roosevelt brought both sides to Portsmouth, New Hampshire. The treaty, signed in September 1905, left Japan in control of Korea, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize for it. That November Japan took over Korea's foreign affairs; in 1910 it took the whole country. The 1904 postcard you'll see in a minute is this moment.

1 · Why Korea matters

The Korean War built America's military, and Toyota.

×3.8
U.S. defense spending, 1950 to 1953
4,679
Toyota trucks ordered by the U.S. Army · 1950–51
Notes

In the spring of 1950 Truman had a plan for a huge military buildup on his desk and no intention of paying for it. Korea paid for it. Defense spending went from $13.7 billion in 1950 to $52.8 billion in 1953, and it never went back to where it was. That's the Cold War military your fathers served in. Across the water, Toyota had been in a management crisis and cutting staff. Two weeks into the war, the Eighth Army asked it for 1,000 trucks; by March 1951 the orders came to 4,679. Japan's car industry restarted on Korea's war.

The myths, and what really happened

2 · Myth: Korea is M*A*S*H

How we learned to misread Korea.

Notes

Show of hands: who learned about Korea from Hawkeye Pierce? Me too. M*A*S*H ran eleven seasons about a war that lasted three, and its 1983 finale drew about 106 million viewers. For a century Korea has been the thing other people fish for, crush, or film.

2 · Myth: The satellite photo says it all

The one picture of Korea everyone knows.

The picture is real. The story told about it is a myth.

Notes

This is the most famous photo of North Korea: the South blazing, the North dark except one dot, Pyongyang. It's real, and it's twelve years old. The myth is what we read into it: that the two Koreas were always this far apart, and that the picture settles which system works, forever. Next slide: for most of the last hundred years they were close, and the North was often ahead.

2 · Myth: The satellite photo says it all

For most of a century, the two Koreas were close.

The big gap is new. It opened around 1990.

Notes

Ups and downs, not a straight line. After the war the North rebuilt faster; even the CIA had it well ahead in the 1960s, and more than 80,000 Koreans in Japan chose to move there. By the early 1980s the two were about even. Then the Soviet Union fell, the North lost its aid and trade, and its income per person dropped by more than a third in eight years. The gap in that satellite photo is about thirty-five years old.

China builds most of the world's ships, Korea most of the rest, and the United States about one-tenth of one percent. So Washington asked Seoul for help, and Korea pledged $150 billion; Hanwha alone is putting $5 billion into the old Philadelphia Navy Yard. Notes

Is Kim crazy? Look at the record from Pyongyang. Iraq had no bomb and was invaded in 2003. Libya gave up its program in December 2003, and in 2011 NATO bombed and Gaddafi was killed. That March, as the bombing started, Pyongyang said the Libya deal had been an "invasion tactic" to disarm the country first. North Korea tested its first bomb in 2006. Today SIPRI estimates about 60 warheads, and last October it paraded a new long-range missile, the Hwasong-20, not yet tested. It's not madness. It's the most rational decision the regime ever made, and no one has invaded.

Notes: likely questions
  • Will North Korea attack? No. A nuclear state that invades the South loses everything it has built. The danger is the border: mines, shootings, accidents.
  • Is Kim crazy? No. He's the most predictable leader in the region. He watched what happened to Saddam and Gaddafi after they gave up their weapons programs, and he kept his.
  • Why do we still have troops there? About 28,500. Deterrence against the North, and America's position facing China. Seoul pays a share of the cost.
  • Do Koreans like us? Yes; support for the alliance runs high. The Georgia raid and the visa fights hurt.
  • Will they reunify? Not soon. In 2024 Pyongyang dropped unification as a goal and now calls the South a separate, hostile state.
